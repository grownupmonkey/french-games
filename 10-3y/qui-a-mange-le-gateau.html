      Pour le prof : how this scene works
      • Every game picks a new culprit and new family details (hair, glasses, T-shirt colour, room, favourite cake flavour). The three clues always point to exactly one person, and the two clues students can see always fit two people. They have to ask about flavours to solve it.
      • Questions are typed. The parser understands common 10-3Y frames, with or without est-ce que or inversion: Comment tu t’appelles ? Tu as quel âge ? Tu aimes le chocolat ? Quel est ton parfum préféré ? Où es-tu ? Où est Hugo ? Tu as faim ? Tu as des lunettes ? People are named by name (Papa, Maman, Hugo, Tante Claire, Oncle Marc, Léa). Unrecognised questions get “Comment ? Je ne comprends pas.” and are counted.
      • The culprit lies about their own favourite flavour. Students have to notice the contradiction and check with other family members, using third-person questions (Quel est le parfum préféré de Léa ? Est-ce que Papa aime le citron ?). Everyone else tells the truth about everyone. Writing a menti or ment in the accusation earns the Détecteur de mensonges badge.
      • The clues: Coco the parrot in the living room repeats the culprit’s hair colour, the empty plate leaves a trail of crumbs to the culprit’s room, and the recipe on the fridge names the cake’s flavour.
      • Answers model the negative with de (Je n’ai pas de lunettes) and il / elle. Missing accents, a missing question mark and missed elisions are pointed out after each question.
      • A wrong accusation gets an in-character alibi tied to a clue (Mais j’ai les cheveux blonds !). Naming two or more clues earns Avec des preuves. A mangé is used as a set phrase; the passé composé itself is 20-3Y.
      • Outcomes practised: 10 C 1.3 (family members), 10 A 3 (seek and give information), 10 R 2.4b (asking questions), 10 R 2.11a (negation), 10 R 2.12 (adjective agreement), 10 R 2.2c (il, elle), 10 R 2.17 (parce que).
      • Audio uses one French voice from this computer: checking…. Nothing is saved or sent anywhere.
